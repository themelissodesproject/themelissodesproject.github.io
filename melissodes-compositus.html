Melissodes compositus

Melissodes compositus is a somewhat uncommon species that occurs mostly within the central-west United States. Although, 6 collections (5 of which recorded during 2016; the other during 1961) from Florida and South Carolina (while the validity of the specimens are unknown) indicates a much larger range than historically assumed. Like all Melissodes, male M. compositus have long antennae, and the females have short antennae in comparison (see "Genus" page for more information). M. compositus, as its name suggests, is likely an oligolege of Asteraceae (historically Compositae), although, currently the lack of floral data isn’t conducive to a justified statement on flower preference. This species resides in the subgenus Callimelissodes, the newest proposed taxonomic rank within Melissodes, and seems to bear the closest resemblance to those of M. lustrus, but shares similarities with M. plumosus and M. metenuus as well. Females can be separated from all other taxa within their subgenus by the deep and crowded mesoscutal punctures (these larger than the scutellar or mesepisternal punctures), the yellow to red hairs on the inner surfaces of the hind basitarsi, the pale lateral thoracic hairs, the light scopae, the basal punctures of the first tergum not surpassing the basal one-third medially (often not even reaching the basal one-third), the all-pale head hairs, and the integument (apart from the terga) bearing fairly “normal” punctures, not distinctly crowded or irregularly abundant (Laberge, 1961). Males are distinctive in that the interbands of the second and third tergum bear many round and deep punctures that are mostly separated by distinctly less than one puncture diameter, the eyes are often bluish green to blue, the apical areas of the second and third terga are glabrous, the penultimate flagellar segment’s length is more than twice its width, and flagellar segments 4-10 (and often 3 and 11 as well) bear dorsolateral, longitudinal, shiny, depressions (see "Description and Identification” for more information on both sexes; Laberge, 1961, 1963). M. compositus, originally described as a subspecies of M. lupinus by Tucker, 1909, was likely given its name as a result of the flower the holotype and possibly paratypes were collected atop. However, true species floral preference should be based on female provisioning efforts (given that males contribute no efforts to brood rearing). Therefore, the current species epithet (originally the subspecific epithet proposed by Tucker, 1909) may not entirely be representative of M. compositus’ diet breadth. Moreover, from the floral data available, while it is suggestive of an Asteraceae specialization with 30 overall records from this family, collections atop Lamiaceae and Tamaricaceae (although very few) have occurred (see “Flower records” for more information).

Description and Identification

Based on Laberge's (1961) description, Melissodes compositus are small setaceous bees (measurements taken from 20 females and 17 males). Females range from 9.5 to 11.0 millimeters in length and 3.0 to 4.5 millimeters in width (width measured at the widest portion of the metasoma). Males are a bit narrower, measuring approximately 9 to 12 millimeters in length and 2.5 to 3.5 millimeters in width (width measured at the widest portion of the metasoma). The female's first flagellar segment is on average 1.94 times the size of the second flagellar segment (standard error 0.025). The males are the opposite where the second flagellar segment is on average 5.05 times the size of the first flagellar segment (standard error 0.143). Female wing length is 3.43 millimeters on average (standard error 0.118 millimeters), and male wing length is 3.27 millimeters on average (standard error 0.116 millimeters). Females have an average of 12.40 hamuli (standard error 0.161), while males have an average of 11.31 (standard error 0.198).

Female

According to Laberge (1961), the description of female M. compositus is as follows. The integument is black, differing at the eyes, which are often gray to bluish-gray; the wing membranes which are colorless hyaline; the wing veins, which are dark brown; the underside of F3-F10, and usually the apical area of F2, which are yellow to reddish beneath; the apical half of the mandibles, which are rufescent; the disitarsi, which are rufescent; and the tibial spurs, which are white to yellow. The clypeus is somewhat shiny and very sparsely shagreened if at all, has no noticeable boss or apical medial carina, and has punctures that are shallow, round, large, and mostly separated by less than, or equal to, half of a puncture’s width. The supraclypeal area is also shiny and very sparsely shagreened if at all and has deep largely separated punctures. The flattened lateral areas of the vertex that reach relatively posterior from, and positioned medially to, the apical portions of the compound eyes are somewhat shiny, but dulled due to distinct reticular shagreening, and has round punctures that are minute and separated by two to four puncture diameters. The ultimate flagellar segment is longer than its width, appearing more rounded than flattened. When looking in facial view, the eyes distinctly converge near the mandibles and are marginally more than one-third as wide as they are long. The four maxillary palpal segments decrease in length from basal to apical in a ratio of approximately 4:3:3:1. The galeae are moderately shiny to shiny, reticularily shagreened in the apical half or more, and have largely spaced punctures with straight hairs arising from them (Fig. 1). The mesoscutum is posteromedially somewhat shiny but dulled due to faint reticular shagreening, prominently so laterally and anteriorly. The mesoscutal punctures are large, and coarse, mostly separated by less than half a puncture diameter and larger than the mesepisternal and scutellar punctures in the areas medial to the parapsidal lines (Fig. 2). Posteromedially, the mesoscutum becomes semi-impunctate to impunctate, and laterally and anteriorly, the punctures are slightly smaller than the medial punctures and separated by less than half a puncture diameter. The scutellum is somewhat shiny but dulled due to delicate shagreening, and has punctures that are moderately smaller than those of the surrounding mesoscutal area, but typically more crowded. The metanotum is matte and dull due to faint tessellation and has punctures that are noticeably smaller than the mesoscutal punctures, and are mostly separated by less than, or equal to, one-half of a puncture’s diameter. The lateral areas of the mesepisterna are shiny with no, or very faint, shagreening and have round shallow punctures that are faintly smaller than the largest punctures on the mesoscutum and mostly separated by less than, or equal to, one puncture diameter (see Fig. 2). The dorsal propodeal surface is reticulorugose apart from the medial area and the rugae in the posterior area of the dorsal surface can partly resemble somewhat indistinct large punctures. The posterior propodeal surface has noticeable round punctures that are separated by one-half to one puncture diameter, apart from the upper impunctate triangle. The lateral propodeal surfaces are matte and dull due to faint minute tessellation and the punctures take on a crowded appearance.

The basal three-fifths of the first tergum have round, shallow, small, punctures that are present both basally and laterally, but only crowded in the basal one-fourth; these punctures are virtually only present in the basal one-third of the first tergum or less, never surpassing this area (Fig. 3). Apart from this area of crowded punctures, the remaining punctures (if present) are mostly separated by two to four puncture diameters. The apical area is impunctate and the tergal surface is dulled due to reticulotransverse shagreening. The second tergum’s basal area is dulled due to dense reticular shagreening and has small round punctures that are mostly separated by approximately half a puncture diameter. The second tergum’s interband zone has shallow irregularly sized scattered punctures that are mostly separated by less than one puncture diameter in the lateral raised areas and mostly separated by two puncture diameters on the medial areas. The apical area is completely impunctate and moderately shiny but reticulotransversely shagreened. The third and fourth terga are similar to that of the second, however, the interband zone punctures are distinct, round, regularly sized, and mostly separated by less than one puncture diameter. The pygidial plate has a U-shape with near-parallel sides in the apical one-third, but divergent at base (Fig. 4).

The female setal description is as follows. The head hair is mostly pale ochraceous to white apart from the dark brown vertex hairs (Fig. 5). The thoracic hairs are laterally pale ochraceous on the lower surfaces, and mostly ochraceous to yellow on the rest of the thorax apart from the scutellum, which has medial dark brown hairs, and the mesoscutum, which has a dark brown posteromedial patch that reaches the medial portion of the tegulae (Fig. 6). The first tergum has ochraceous basal hairs, but the apical area is glabrous. The second tergum’s basal area has white to pale ochraceous pubescence, and the distal band is instead reduced to lateral short, oblique, pale fasciae that reaches, or almost reaches, the apical margin at extreme lateral areas. The basal band is connected to this lateral fasciae by pale pubescence. The apical area of the second tergum is glabrous, but the interband zone has suberect to appressed, simple, pale brown, tiny, scattered hairs. The third tergum is similar to that of the second, except the basal hairs are brown, the distal band is usually complete with no medial interruption, the apical area is shorter (approximately one-half of the medial area of the distal band), and the interband zone is instead covered in pale, diffused, appressed, hairs. The fourth tergum is similar to that of the third, except the distal band reaches the apex across the entire tergum and is always complete. The fifth and sixth terga are laterally white, and medially often pale brown to golden (Fig. 7). The sternal hairs are white laterally and become more gold-colored medially. The leg hairs are mostly white, differing at the outer surfaces of the fore basitarsi, which are brown; the inner surfaces of the tarsi, which are orange to yellow; and often the inner surfaces of the hind basitarsi, which are reddish to yellow (Fig. 8).

Male

According to Laberge (1961), the male M. compositus description is as follows. The integument is black, differing at the mandibular bases, clypeus, and labrum, with the labrum having its margin not darkened, which are yellow; the eyes, which are bluish gray to gray, and on rare occasions, greenish blue (Fig. 9); the underside flagellar segments 2-11, which are red to yellow (these segments are dark brown on top); the wing membranes, which are hyaline; the wing veins, which are black to brown; terga 2-5, which are usually yellow-brown hyaline apically; the distitarsi, which are rufescent; and the tibial spurs, which are white to yellow. In facial view, the eyes noticeably converge near the mandibles and, in length, measure to be around slightly longer than 3 times their width. The first flagellar segment’s minimum length is more than one-fifth of the second segment’s maximum length. Flagellar segments 4-10 (sometimes also on 11 and 3) have shiny, shallow, longitudinal dorsolateral depressions (Fig. 10). The length of the penultimate segments is more than twice, but less than three times, its width (Fig. 11) and the ultimate segment’s length is approximately three times its width. The four maxillary palpal segments are in a ratio of approximately 4:3:3:1, with the last segment occasionally shorter. The fourth sternum is medially broad and shallowly emarginate, or evenly convex across the sterna, and the fifth and third sterna are noticeably apically convex (Fig. 12). The galeae are somewhat shiny above, but dulled due to faint reticular shagreening, minimally in the apical one-half and sometimes more. The remainder of the sculptural characteristics are similar to that of the females except as follows: the posteromedial mesoscutal area usually has some scattered punctures; the basal four-fifths to five-sixths of the first tergum is coarsely punctate with irregularly spaced punctures, mostly separated by less than one to three puncture diameters; the second tergum’s interband zone has large round punctures (similarly sized to the basal punctures of T1) that are mostly separated by distincly less than, or somtimes equal to, one puncture diameter (Fig. 13); the third tergum is similar to that of the second, however, the interband punctures are more crowded (see Fig. 13); the fourth and fifth terga are similar to that of the third.

The medial plate of the seventh sternum isn’t laterally twisted but rather flat (not facing laterally) and the lateral portions of its apical area create a rounded angle, but before the apex of the rounded angle a distinct narrowing occurs, forming a semi-necked area that widens into a capitate knob. The ventral area of this neck and knob bear short hairs, be it fewer than that of M. plumosus. The membranous area medial to the lateral plate of the seventh sternum and below the medial plate of the same sternum is often smaller than that of M. plumosus. The apical/distal margin of the eighth sternum bears many minute hairs and the ventral tubercle in the medial areas of this same sternum is often unidentate, becoming apically rounded. The gonostyli are laterally glabrous but bear scarce short ventral hairs basally, capitate, and their lengths measure to be less than two thirds of length of the gonocoxite. The length of the spatha is approximately one-third of its width, and this structure is apically sinuate, be it less than that of M. plumosus. The penis valve has a prominent dorsolateral lamella; the basal end of the lamella ends in an inflected tooth near the spatha.

The male setal description is as follows. The head hairs are mostly dark ochraceous to pale ochraceous apart from the vertex, which often has some darker hairs. The thoracic hairs are also mostly dark ochraceous to pale ochraceous apart from the dorsal areas, which tend to bear darker hairs. The tergal hairs are mostly pale ochraceous to white, with the second, third, and fourth terga having glabrous apical areas (Fig. 14); the fifth tergum, which reaches the apex across the entire tergum; and the sixth and seventh terga, which have some pale brown to dark ochraceous hairs, predominantly medially and basally. The legs have white to pale ochraceous hairs, differing at the inner surfaces of the tarsi, which are pale orange to yellow.

Location and Habitat

Melissodes compositus is a somewhat common to fairly uncommon species that ranges across the majority of the west-central United States (Laberge 1961), but has recently been collected in Florida and South Carolina; an extreme range enlargement to that of what's been historically recognized. In his revision, Laberge (1961) reported M. compositus’ range to include the following states: Montana, Utah, Idaho, New Mexico, Arizona, Colorado, and Wyoming; the last state falling into the illustrated range in Fig. 7 in Laberge’s publication, but yielding no records. A similar distribution can be seen today, however, as said above, the presence of this species in Florida (Ikerd, 2019; GBIF record 658943753) and South Carolina (Gibbs, 2026; GBIF record 4540093136) is indicative of a distribution that includes a large section of central and eastern United States (Fig. 16). In addition to the locations listed by Laberge (1961) and explained above, M. compositus has also been found in Nevada, definitively so in Wyoming, and in California (state records are listed below), and possibly occurs throughout portions of South Dakota, Nebraska, Kansas, Oklahoma, Missouri, Arkansas, Southern Illinois, South Western Iowa, Western Kentucky, Northern Louisiana, Northern Texas, Oregon, Tennessee, Alabama, and Georgia. However, the lack of collections from the latter list leads to merely a speculative presence in these states. Although believed to be an Asteraceae oligolege, with no clear preference on one specific floral genus (it's unknown whether this is reflective of true diet or lack of data; see flower records for more information), an assessment of habitat based on host habitat cannot be provided. M. compositus has been collected throughout the months of May and October, seemingly with peak activity (at least based on presence-only data) occurring during August and September; a phenology somewhat later in the year in comparison to the average peak of the genus (Fig. 17).

Bionomics

Unfortunately, no current studies have concerned themselves with the nesting biology, behaviors, or ecology of M. compositus. Therefore, all that is known in regards to the foregoing topics in relation to M. compositus is that of which is known for the genus. From the current available data (231 records, 59 of which bear elevation data), this species seems to occupy elevations ranging between 1101m to 6300m. While some species in the genus (e.g. M. apicatus) seem to be obligated to nest in certain environmental features that are abiotic (i.e. obligated to nesting in sand), the vast distribution of M. compositus is suggestive of the opposite. Furthermore, given that the floral records presented below (be it few records exist) show no clear genus of preference but likely a specialization on Asteraceae, and with this floral family having an incredibly extensive distribution, the location of M. compositus’ nests are likely dependant upon the availability of their food rather than then soil. Recent studies of another Melissodes’ (M. agilis) foraging behavior estimated that the range in which females travel to provision their nests falls between 14 and 16 meters (Foy, 2025), and with the literature previously establishing that females nest in the soils in which their floral-hosts grow (Parker et al., 1983), the assumption of M. compositus exhibiting no soil obligation appears more justified than the inverse.

Flower records

Although the current floral data of M. compositus is sparse, and therefore the true preference and/or specialization of this species is still unknown, Laberge (1961) categorized this species as a probable Asteraceae oligolege. However, in 2020, Wright et al’s study on the evolution of diet breadth of Melissodes suggests that this species is instead a generalist. Using genetic analysis of pollen from 14 M. compositus specimens, Wright et al (2020) found that their dataset included pollen from 3 different floral families, 4 different tribes, and 8 genera (6 of the 8 genera belonging to Asteraceae). While this is quite significant given that Laberge’s (1961) data included only taxa from Asteraceae, the paucity of Wright et al’s (2020) pollen data and somewhat small sample size (similar to how sparse the records are now) may over-inflate, or under-inflate, the level of generalism that M. compositus exhibits. While not genetically backed, a list of current flower records and their collection frequencies is presented below (Figs. 18 & 19). As expressed by Wright et al. (2020), much of the genetic material within their dataset was found to differ with the taxon assigned on the label. This may impact the floral record presented below given that all records are based on the observer’s knowledge of flower identification.

Taxonomy and Phylogeny

Although not nearly as historically taxonomically dense as others in the genus (e.g. M. communis, M. bimaculatus, or M. rivalis), M. compositus has been reclassifed twice. M. compositus was first described in 1909 by Elbert S. Tucker as the variant of M. lupinus, M. l. composita. Three male specimens comprise the type material from which Tucker’s description is based, each one described to have “...eyes greenish...” and “...basal (abdominal) segment clothed with long, sparse, white pubescence, subfasciate on apical margin for entire length in case it has not been denuded; second to fourth segments each with a subapical fascia of whitish appressed but rather loose pubescence, curved inwardly at middle but reaching apical margin at sides...” Given that all the collections of, what was at the time considered to be, M. l. compostia are of the male sex, and that flagellar ratios along with pubescence were used as primary identifying factors, one can see how M. compositus and M. lupinus males may look very similar in these regards (however, females look incredibly different on the account of the distal flagellar segment’s structure). Puncture descriptions, and those of microsculpting apart from remarks of the shiny mesoscutum, also lacked in Tucker’s (1909) description; these characters bearing essential weight in the classification of M. compositus in Laberge’s (1961 & 1963) keys. On collection of these males, Tucker (1909) noted that he sent them to T. D. A. Cockerell for examination and comparisons against the latter’s M. lupinus specimens. Therefore, the logic as to why these males were originally considered to be only a variety of M. lupinus is derived from Cockerell’s (1909) publication. Cockerell noted that Tucker’s males are extremely similar to those of M. lupinus (from his reference material), differing most notably in the eyes, which are “bluer green and diverge less above,” and the mesothorax, which is “more shiny”. Characters such as the former, that is bluer eyes, can be seen as traits used in Laberge’s (1961) key to separate M. compositus from M. lustrus (among other characters), however, the morphological features used for current day identification are ones often overlooked in the historical literature. Males of M. compositus can readily be identified from those of M. lupinus in the former bearing longitudinal dorsolateral depressions on flagellar segments 4-10 (sometimes on 11 as well, and only weakly developed on 3 if present), whereas the latter often bears depressions on segments 5-9 (sometimes 10, but never 11 or 3). These structures can, however, overlap in both males (be it fairly uncommon) bearing depressions on segments 4-10. In these cases, M. compositus can be separated from M. lupinus by the glabrous apical areas of terga 2-4, and by the first flagellar segment’s minimum length equaling more than one-fifth of the second segment’s maximum length. The most important characteristics for preliminary species delimitation, likely the features Laberge (1961) used to definitely separate these species before secondary characteristics (i.e. vestiture, external structure, and sculpture) were established, are those of the male genitalic structures. M. compositus and M. lupinus differ distinctly in the medial plate of the seventh sternum, the hairs present on the apex of the eighth sternum, and the length of the Gonostyli, these differences likely resulting in their current classifications. The holotype male collected by Tucker (1909) in Colorado Spring, Colorado in August of 1906 resides in the U. S. Nation Museum (Laberge, 1961). The two paratype males collected on the same outing as the holotype are deposited in the University of Kansas’ Snow Entomological Collection (Laberge, 1991).

After its promotion from variant to species, M. compositus was subsequently renamed due to the introduction of the International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature’s (ICZN) fourth edition. The release, in 1999, included article 30.1.4.4 which states if a genus with the suffix -odes (i.e. Melissodes) was not assigned a specific gender by the author who proposed it, then the genus becomes masculine. When Melissodes was proposed in 1829 by Latreille, he included no statements deeming the name “Melissodes” to be feminine or masculine, therefore, the genus became masculine. According to ICZN, species and genus names must make gender agreement. Because the suffix -a (i.e. composita) is feminine and the genus name is now masculine, the species name also had to be updated with a masculine suffix; the suffix -us is masculine hence “compositus” instead of “composita”.

Phylogeny

In Laberge’s (1961) genus-wide key developed to identify specimens to subspecific ranks, females of M. compositus would identify to the subgenus Callimelissodes on the eighth couplet along with those of M. lustrus and M. compositus. Interestingly, these three species seem to be the only taxa that bear light thoracic hairs and have the punctures on the basal area of the first tergum restricted to the basal one-third. The subgenus in which M. compositus resides was originally withheld in the larger Eumelissodes subgenus during the first two parts of Laberge’s revisions. This, as noted by the Melissodes revisionist, was due to a lack of distinctive shared female characters shared amongst the taxa within this clade. Males of this subgenus on the other hand each exhibit a convex margin on the fourth sternum, the subgeneric trait that classifies specimens of this sex as being Callimelissodes. While a dissonance in the number of subgeneric characters between the two sexes that classify a taxon as falling into Callimelissodes still exists (there still currently being no real trait that readily identifies females of Callimelissodes from Eumelissodes), in his 1961 revision, Laberge formally recognized the subgenus, including within it 14 species. Presumably, the males were first added to Callimelissodes on account of the fourth sternum and then the corresponding females (either identified as being the correct female by the distributions, collections with males, or genitalic examination) were referenced to build the somewhat convoluted path that is the female key to the subgenera of Melissodes. Sixth couplets comprise all terminating paths that lead to Callimelissodes in Laberge’s (1961) female key, and with 14 species falling into this subgenus, that totals on average one couplet accounting for 2.3 species (14/6). For contrast, in the largest subgenus, Eumelissodes (and what would presumably have the largest variability), on average, each couplet accounts for 9 species (72/8). This pattern is quite strange as one would expect that males of such distinctiveness would in turn result in their female counterparts exhibiting a similar distinctive trait, somewhat suggestive of a specific clade within Eumelissodes rather than a separate subgenus based on morphology alone (morphology being, at the time, the only material from which Laberge’s taxonomic reclassifications were based). However, in 2020, Wright et al. conducted a phylogenetic analysis of 89 Melissodes species based molecular data (unknown whether males and females were used), which resulted in a highly distinct clade containing all 12 included Callimelissodes, solidifying Laberge’s (1961) inclusion of Callimelissodes as a true subgenus. All three of the species that are identified from the eighth couplet of Laberge’s (1961) key were included in this genetic analysis (Fig. 19), but the degrees of relatedness among M. compositus, M. lustrus, and M. glenwoodensis are unexpected. Based on Wright et al’s (2020) findings, M. compositus is genetically most closely related to M. tuckeri, and M. lustrus and M. glenwoodensis are most closely related to one another (see Fig. 12). As can be seen in the similar taxa comparisons below, M. compositus and M. lustrus are undoubtedly more similar in their morphologies than M. glenwoodensis is to either of the foregoing species. Wright et al.’s (2020) phylogenetic reconstruction of the genus using molecular data has changed how the relationships among Melissodes are viewed in that morphologies, while still incredibly important in identification, don’t necessarily reflect the true relatedness of species.

Similar Taxa

With males falling into the subgenus Callimelissodes, separation from all other subgenera of Melissodes is fairly simple (i.e. by the convex structure of the fourth sternum). Therefore, males only resemble those in their subgenus, and specifically, M. compositus is regarded to bear strong resemblance to those of M. lustrus. Females of Callimelissodes, on the other hand, as expressed by Laberge (1961), are more challenging to identify than those of males, and as shown in the phylogeny section above, share no single characteristic that defines them as Callimelissodes. Although the subgenus still lacks distinctive and shared characters amongst all females, in Laberge’s (1961) key to the subgenera of Melissodes, M. compositus females would be identified to Callimelissodes on the eighth couplet, as would M. lustrus and M. glenwoodensis, due to the basal punctures on the first tergum never surpassing the basal one-third (often falling short of this length) and the pale lateral thoracic hairs (specimens of such punctation patterns with dark lateral thoracic hair would key to M. metenuus). Despite the fact that M. compositus’ description has already been treated above, a subsequent comparison of diagnostic differences of both sexes among the foregoing species may shed light on some important differences. It is of interest that, as expounded upon below, while females of M. compositus are similar to those of M. glenwoodenis in the sculpture of the first tergum, males are distinctly different in many characters.

The distributions of M. compositus, M. lustrus, and M. glenwoodensis overlap in New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming, Idaho, and California (see citation block below). Additionally, M. compositus and M. glenwoodensis share the states of Montana, Colorado, and Arizona (see citation block below), however, the latter is much easier to separate from M. compositus than M. lustrus. All of these species share a very similar phenology and floral preference, most with peak activity occurring around the latter part of August and throughout September, likely with a preference of Asteraceae (see species treatments of M. lustrus and M. glenwoodensis for more information). For these reasons, comparisons focusing purely on diagnostic traits will be given below. All morphological comparisons are derived from the works of Laberge (1961 & 1963).

Female

Females of M. compositus are incredibly distinct purely based on the basal puncture pattern of the first tergum coupled with the entirely light thoracic hairs. This, as explained above, is shared by only two other species, one of which differs fairly substantially from M. compositus and is therefore easily identified from the former (M. glenwoodensis), the other bearing an extremely strong resemblance to M. compositus in both sexes (M. lustrus). Females of M. compositus can be separated from those of M. glenwoodensis by the often smaller size (M. compositus ranging between 9.5-11mm whereas M. glenwoodensis ranges between 11-14mm) and the reddish to yellow hairs on the inner surfaces of the hind basitarsi that do not distinctly contrast with the rest of the scopae (Fig. 20). Furthermore, M. glenwoodensis females often have yellow scopae and black to dark brown hairs on the fifth and sixth terga (pale lateral tufts may or may not be present), whereas M. compositus females have pale ochraceous to white scopae and golden to pale brown medial hairs on the fifth and sixth terga (large white lateral tufts always present; Fig. 21). However, while these characters can be used to separate M. compositus from M. glenwoodensis, M. lustrus and the former share these traits (and many others). Separation of M. compositus from M. lustrus is more challenging than from M. glenwoodensis, the two only diognostically differing in the punctation of the mesoscutum. The punctures on the mesoscutum of M. compositus that are located medial to the parapsidal line are mostly separated by equal to, or less than, one-half of a puncture diameter, deep, and mostly greater in size than those of the mesepisternal or scutellar punctures (Fig. 22). For contrast, the mesoscutal punctures medial to the parapsidal line on M. lustrus are mostly separated by more than one-half of a puncture’s width (many of these separated by more than, or equal to, two puncture diameters), shallow, and irregularly sized, although, most are smaller than those of the mesepisternal or scutellar punctures.

Male

Unlike females, males exhibit very little resemblance to those of M. glenwoodensis, the latter taxon following a similar path in Laberge’s (1961 & 1963) keys to that of M. lupinus based on the antennal depressions. When identifying similar taxa of males of M. compositus, overwhelming similarities arise in M. lustrus. Although these species don’t share the highest possible relation to one anther genetically, M. compositus and M. lustrus are incredibly closely related morphologically; both bearing depressions on flagellar segments 4-10 (often 11 as well and if present on 3, then only scarcely developed), have the their penultimate segments’ lengths’ twice their width’s, have comparatively shiny galeae, and glabrous apical areas on the second and third terga. M. compositus can somtimes be separated from M. lustrus by the eyes, which are usually bluish green to blue in the former whereas the eye color of the latter is greenish yellow to green (see Fig. 9). However, the two species can be more definitively separated from one another by the punctures on the interband zones of the second and third terga. In M. compositus, these punctures are mostly separated by distinctly less than one puncture diameter, whereas in M. lustrus, these punctures are mostly separated by one puncture diameter with many separated by more than two to sometimes as much as three puncture diameters (Fig. 23). M. glenwoodensis, as explained above, bears less similarities to M. compositus than M. lustrus does, and can readily be identified from the former by the lack of a flagellar depression on the fourth segment (Fig. 24), the sixth and seventh terga often being shrouded in dark brown hairs (Fig. 25), and by the larger size.
