How Efficient Are Bees in Pollinating Sunflowers?

Parker, F. D. (1981). Journal of the Kansas Entomological Society.

This field study at Logan, Utah compares pollination efficiency of native bees versus honey bees on sunflower, finding Melissodes agilis (both sexes) to be the most effective and most faithful pollinator across all sterile/fertile line combinations, producing far more seeds per visit than Apis mellifera and carrying substantially more pollen (up to ~104,500 grains per female) than any other bee group studied.
The relative efficiencies of native bees and honey bees in pollinating sunflower were compared with male sterile and male fertile lines. Wild bees as a group and some oligolectic females were more effective in pollinating florets than were the apids (honey bees and bumble bees). In comparisons of the fidelity of bee visitation to either sterile or pollen varieties, apids preferred male sterile rows, some wild bee species were evenly distributed between sterile and pollen rows, and other wild bees preferred pollen rows. In comparisons of the amount of pollen carried on a bee body, oligolectic sunflower bees carried more pollen than did other kinds of bees.

Parker Bee Biology and Systematics Laboratory, Agricultural Research, Science and Education Administration, USDA, Logan, Utah 84322 ABSTRACT: The relative efficiencies of native bees and honey bees in pollinating sunflower were compared with male sterile and male fertile lines. Wild bees as a group and some oligolectic females were more effective in pollinating florets than were the apids (honey bees and bumble bees). In comparisons of the fidelity of bee visitation to either sterile or pollen varieties, apids preferred male sterile rows, some wild bee species were evenly distributed between sterile and pollen rows, and other wild bees preferred pollen rows. In comparisons of the amount of pollen carried on a bee body, oligolectic sunflower bees carried more pollen than did other kinds of bees. Few researchers have examined the role of bees in the production of sunflower seed since this plant has become an important oil seed crop (Carter, 1978). In fact, so little is known concerning the diversity of bee species and their relative efficiency as pollinators of this crop that most commercial sunflower producers use self-fertile varieties (Fick and Rehder, 1977). Some researchers have suggested the use of honey bees, Apis mellifera L., as pollinators, but the recommended numbers of colonies vary from 1 to 5/hectare (McGregor, 1976). The sunflower is a native North American crop species with which many native bee species have probably co-evolved (Hurd et al., 1980). Many species of native bees are commonly observed associated with commercial plantings of sunflower, but their role in seed production has not been investigated. Parker (1980) found that sunflower oligoleges (bees restricted in pollen utilization to sunflower or closely related composites) carried more pollen on their body hairs than did sunflower polyleges (bees that visit many crop species including sunflower). However, relative effectiveness of these groups as sunflower pollinators was not determined in his study. This study was conducted 1) to compare different species of native bees with honey bees as pollinators of sunflowers, and 2) to compare the fidelity of bee species to male fertile or male sterile lines (most commercial self-fertile varieties are produced by hybridization of male sterile and male fertile lines, and bees are the mechanism of pollen transfer). [Footnote: Received for publication 24 May 1980.] Methods and Materials PLOTS: Sunflower was grown at the North Farm at Logan, Utah in Millville silt loam. Northrup King Co., Woodland, California, supplied 6 male sterile lines (SW 504, 506, 509, 517, 526) and 1 male fertile line (RW 637). Twenty-four rows (.7-m apart and 16-m long) were planted. Rows 1, 2, 9, 16, 23, and 24 were sown with the male fertile line and the remaining 3 blocks (rows 3-8, 10-15, and 17-22) were divided in half and sown with the 6 male sterile lines. COUNTS: Bloom began on August 13 and ended on September 4. Pollinators were counted and changes in abundance and diversity of pollinators noted on early blooming heads 3 times daily (9:00 am, 11:00 am, and 1:00 pm) on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays to determine any changes in abundance and diversity of pollinators during the day. Just prior to bloom, 20 randomly chosen plants of each variety were bagged with cheesecloth to exclude insect visitors; when these heads were in full bloom, the bags were removed to allow bee visitation. After one bee visit/head, the bag was replaced to prevent additional visits. During each visit, a sketch of the flower and the route taken by the bee on the florets was made (Fig. 1). To determine if pollen was accidentally transferred with the bagging technique, we treated 10 heads as above except that no bees were allowed to land before the flower was rebagged. Any male fertile plants in the sterile plots were rogued. However, the actual number of heads used was 86; some heads were unvisited and others were fertile instead of male sterile. Data on single visits/head were lumped because of inconsistent bee visitation/variety and similarities among varieties in percentage seed set when open-pollinated. HARVESTING: In October, we collected all bagged heads and recorded the number and pattern of mature seed set by opening the seed. Mature (pollinated) seed was easily distinguished by size from immature (unpollinated) seed (Fig. 2). We determined seed yield/head of unbagged heads by sampling 12 heads/variety. Each head was sampled by removal of 6 arcs (a single row of seed from the outer edge of a head to the center) of seed at the 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 o'clock positions (Fig. 3). The number, percentage, and weight of the mature seed in each arc were recorded. An additional 102 heads (17/row) sampled randomly from each plot were used to estimate yield/hectare. POLLEN: To estimate the amount of pollen carried on a bee body, we collected samples of bees from male sterile and male fertile lines by catching a single bee in a long tube, adding distilled water, vigorously shaking the vial up and down until no more pollen could be seen on the bee, and then removing the bee. Only one female (Megachile parallela Smith) died when treated in this manner. After the bees dried sufficiently, they flew off, and many treated bees were seen a short time later gathering nectar from sunflower heads. The washed pollen was centrifuged in the vial, and the number of pollen grains/insect was counted with a hemocytometer. Figs. 1-3. 1. Pattern of bee visit on male sterile sunflower. 2. Results The bee species studied differed in their ability to pollinate sunflowers. Visits by polylectic apids (honey bees and bumble bees) produced the lowest number of seeds, whereas visits by females of the sunflower oligoleges (Andrena helianthi Robertson and Melissodes agilis Cresson) produced the highest number of seeds (Table 1). A majority of single honey bee visits (58%) resulted in no seed set. A somewhat lower percentage of unpollinated florets resulted from visits of male Agapostemon sp. (31%) and male Melissodes (29%). Female oligoleges of sunflower produced 6 or more seeds/visit. The percentage of single Apis visits (N = 22) that resulted in 0-5 or ≥6 seeds/insect visit was compared with percentages of all other native bees combined (N = 43); visits by native individuals were significantly more likely to produce ≥6 seeds than were honey bee visits (χ² = 4.58, d.f. = 1, P > .05). The amount of pollen carried on the body hairs of bees collected from male sterile lines varied considerably among species. Males of Melissodes and honey bee workers carried similar amounts of pollen. Female oligoleges, however, carried more pollen than did any other group of bees (Table 2). Differences in visitation patterns among bee species were found when male sterile and male fertile lines were compared. Apids were rarely found on the male fertile rows; only 1% of the honey bee and none of the bumble bee visits were recorded on pollen rows. Conversely, few Andrena (3%) were recorded on the male sterile rows. Other less abundant wild bees (Halictus ligatus Say, Perdita albipennis Cresson, Pseudopanurgus aethiops (Cresson), and Pterosarus albitarsis (Cresson), had visitation patterns similar to Andrena (Table 3). Melissodes, the most common wild bee, visited all sterile lines in comparable numbers. The Melissodes population during the 1979 season was only slightly lower than that of honey bees, 2405 Melissodes and 3051 honey bees (unpublished data). Other less abundant wild bees such as Agapostemon sp., Halictus farinosus Smith, and Megachile parallela Smith were evenly distributed among the sterile and fertile rows. More wild bees were seen on the pollen rows than on the sterile rows. When all sterile varieties were compared, only 1 (SW 517) had fewer bee visits. The percentage of florets/head that produced seeds on the unbagged heads for each sterile variety was SW 504, 57.0%; SW 506, 66.4%; SW 509, 62.8%; SW 514, 55.2%; SW 527, 49.8%; SW 526, 61.1%. In contrast, this study suggests that honey bees are among the least efficient pollinators of male sterile sunflower. Because honey bees did not frequently visit the pollen rows, they carried less pollen on their body hair than did female oligolectic bees. Also, honey bees were observed grooming sunflower pollen from their bodies. These results confirm those of an earlier study, in which honey bees were found to be less effective than wild bees as pollinators of high and low self-fertile varieties of commercial oil-seed sunflower simply because of their inconsistent visitation pattern (Parker, 1980). In the 1979 studies an average male sterile sunflower head produced 600 mature seeds. This amount of seed, considering plant density and size, would produce between 1791 and 4960 kg/hectare, depending on variety. Those varieties with a higher rate of honey bee visitation, SW 504 and SW 509, did not produce more seed than those with a lower rate of honey bee visitation. The two highest yielding varieties, SW 506 with 4960 kg/hectare and SW 526 with 3080 kg/hectare, only received, respectively, 15 and 5% of the total honey bee visitation. However, both high yielding varieties were frequently visited by female Melissodes (Table 3). In this study and in the previous study (Parker, 1980), Melissodes was the most important pollinator because it commonly visited all varieties. Single visits by either sex resulted in pollination of numerous florets, and it was the most abundant wild bee. Male Melissodes were important pollinators also, because they were numerous, they carried many pollen grains on their bodies, they spent most of the day flying from flower to flower in their incessant search for females, and they often landed on the flower heads (64% of their visits lasted longer than 1 second). Also, these males spent the night in clusters on the flower heads. The percentage of florets that produced seed in these studies averaged 59% for all varieties combined. Most varieties were visited as often as 150 times/day. When the number of florets available per day, the amount of pollen transferred, and the potential seed set/bee visit are considered, the percentage of florets pollinated and producing seed in this study was lower than expected. Perhaps this amount of seed is all that these plants were capable of maturing, and the true effect of bees as potential pollinators was underestimated. The influence of native bees on sunflower yields has not been previously considered. Because native bees such as Melissodes and Andrena are capable of setting from 4.9 to 10.9 times as many seeds/visit as are honey bees on male sterile varieties (their relative efficacy on male fertile lines has not yet been compared), they are important sunflower pollinators even though their presence is less obvious than that of honey bees. No one has yet tried to manage populations of any native bee for sunflower pollination. Thus, the management and use of some species of native bees as commercial pollinators of sunflower may have an influence on future sunflower seed production, especially since the average production/hectare in the U.S. (1460 kg) has been less than that produced by any of the varieties used in these studies.
