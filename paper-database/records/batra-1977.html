Nests of Centris, Melissodes, and Colletes in Guatemala (Hymenoptera: Apoidea)

Batra, S. W. T., Schuster, J. C. (1977). Biotropica.

This paper gives one of the few available nest descriptions for the genus Melissodes, documenting a complete nest of Melissodes floris (subgenus Eumelissodes) found within a dense multi-species bee aggregation on a roadside bank near San Miguel Duenas, Guatemala. It reports burrow and cell dimensions, provision character, and notes that three dead M. floris females each carried a puparium of a phorid fly (Phoridae) in the thorax, indicating parasitism of the bee by this fly.
An uncommon interspecific dense aggregation of nests constructed by the solitary bees Centris transversa, C. anthracina, Melissodes floris, Colletes sp., and Anthophora sp. occupied a steep bank in Guatemala. The nests, cells, provisions, and brood of the first four species are described and illustrated for the first time. A Centris inquiline was Coelioxys sp. (Megachilidae); Melissodes females were parasitized by phorid flies.

THE NESTING HABITS OF BEES of the genera Centris, Melissodes, and Colletes are relatively little known, and nothing is known of the nests of the species listed below. Information on nests in Guatemala is therefore presented here. Centris is a western hemisphere, primarily neotropical genus of large to medium-sized anthophorid bees (Centridini); Melissodes is a large genus of medium-sized nearctic and neotropical anthophorid bees (Eucerini); and Colletes is a large, widespread genus of medium-sized to small bees (Colletidae: Colletinae). Near San Miguel Duenas, Department of Sacatepequez, Guatemala, at 1730 m elevation on the slopes of Acatenango Volcano, an east-facing roadside bank was found riddled with nests of the following bees (listed in descending order of abundance): Centris (Hemisiella) transversa Perez, Centris (Paracentris) anthracina Snelling, Melissodes (Eumelissodes) floris Cockerell, Colletes sp. nr. C. xerophilus Timberlake, and Anthophora sp. nr. A. californica Cresson. The nearly vertical, 6 m high bank was composed of layers of fine loess-like soil and volcanic ash. The dominant vegetation in the vicinity consisted of bushy second growth with coffee planted nearby. The area is classified as Subtropical Wet Forest according to L. R. Holdridge (undated). The bees' nests were examined on 28 November and 2 December 1975, at the beginning of the dry season. Although aggregations of nests of a single species are fairly common, dense aggregations of several species of bees are unusual (see Rau 1926, Michener et al. 1958, Batra 1966). All strata of the bank contained remnants of nests of previous years as well as the currently occupied nests. Many old and some occupied burrows and cells were exposed due to erosion of the soft, dry soil of the bank face. Numerous bees (primarily female Centris transversa) were flying back and forth in front of the bank, intermittently entering holes or crevices. Evidently these were seeking nesting sites. Prolonged buzzing sounds were distinctly audible 5 m from the bank as nest-occupying bees defended their nests against intruders. Provisioning and flight activity ceased by about 4 P.M. Those females without nests slept in exposed old cells, holes, or crevices. Bombyliid and muscoid flies (not collected) that were hovering about the bank were probably parasites or inquilines of the bees. NESTS OF Centris transversa.--Females of Centris transversa facultatively constructed new burrows running horizontally into the bank (up to 6.5 cm long x 6 mm diam., fig. 1) or more often used exposed old cells in lieu of a burrow (fig. 2). The 35 nests examined contained 1 to 5 cells. The cells, usually oriented vertically or somewhat slanting, were mainly found within the outer 10 cm of the bank. Males of C. transversa were patrolling a watering site, so mating may occur there rather than at the nest site where males were not found. However, according to Raw (1975), males of Jamaican Centris spp. maintain territories among plants, which are marked with scent. The cells were constructed of a 0.5 to 2.0 mm thick layer of very hard, firm, somewhat shiny dark-brown material that appeared to be a mixture of mud and a waxy or resinous substance. According to Michener and Lange (1958), Centris spp. in Panama collect mud on their scopae for nest construction. Vesey-FitzGerald (1939) noted that cells of Centris spp. in Trinidad are lined with a "wax-like substance" that is initially moist, and that soaks into and binds the dirt particles while it is hardening. The cell lining of Centris derasa Lepeletier is waterproof (Bennett 1964). Centris cineraria Smith of Chile resembles C. transversa in its habit of either restoring old cells or making new galleries (Claude-Joseph 1926). Several species of Centris nest in occupied termite carton (Vesey-FitzGerald 1939, Bennett 1964, Pickel 1928); many nest in banks, level ground, or mud walls. Centris lanipes Fabricius makes mud nests in holes in logs in Brazil (Michener and Lange 1958), and C. atriventris Mocsary makes its nests in rain-forest mounds of the ant, Atta cephalotes (Linnaeus), in Trinidad (Vesey-FitzGerald 1928). The cells of C. transversa in Guatemala measured 14-17 mm (outside length), 10-19 mm (inner length), 9-13 mm (outer diameter), and 7-10 mm (inner diameter). The necks of the cells were 4.5 to 6.0 mm wide. After provisioning, the cells were closed by caps of a substance resembling the material of which the cells were made except that it was of a somewhat lighter color. These caps (fig. 7) had an eccentric depression from which fine streaks radiated; perhaps they were formed by the bee's tongue during cell closure. Claude-Joseph (1926) noted similar cell caps in two species of Centris. The provisions, which were olive green, semiliquid, and filled the lowermost 5-8 mm of the cells, had a faint honeylike fragrance; no yeasts were isolated from them. The 3 mm long egg (fig. 8a) floated on top of the provisions. Feeding larvae (fig. 7) lay curled on one side and apparently pushed the edges of the provision upward as they moved about in the cell. Mature larvae (fig. 8c) oriented longitudinally in cells and deposited 8mm long fecal streaks around the lower part of the cells. Centris larvae, unlike larvae of many other bees, did not move about when touched or exposed to light. The prepupa, however, was responsive. Freshly spun cocoon silk was transparent, but it became brownish pink to reddish within four days. Older cocoons were chestnut brown with an apical nipple. According to Claude-Joseph (1926) C. cineraria and C. nigerrima remain in the cells for two years. The development of C. transversa also seems to be slow. All brood stages, including a few pupae, were found, but the youngest stages were predominant. One cell contained a predaceous second-instar larva of the megachilid bee, Coelioxys sp. (fig. 9), that evidently had hatched from an egg inserted through a hole in the cell at the surface of the provision; no Centris egg was present, and the larva had consumed some pollen. An adult female Coelioxys was reared from another cell. Centris transversa, a Central American species, was collected once in Arizona, U.S.A., on tamarisk (Snelling 1966). NESTS OF Centris anthracina.--Nests and brood of Centris anthracina were larger but otherwise similar to those of C. transversa. However, old cells apparently were not reoccupied. The horizontal 11-13 mm wide burrows of 10 nests were 6 to 17 cm long, and each ended in a single vertical to subvertical cell (fig. 3). Cells were 15-17 mm long (inner length) by 10.0-11.5 mm in inner diameter. The 0.5-1.5 mm thick walls of the cells resembled those of C. transversa. The cells were so strong that they could be repeatedly dropped from a height of 3 m to a hard floor without breaking or cracking. The dark, olive-green, semiliquid provision occupied the lower 5 mm of the cell, and the 4.5 x 1.2 mm curved egg was centered on its surface. This species ranges from Mexico to Ecuador (Snelling 1966). It pollinates Cassia quiedondilla Micheli in Mexico, vibrating or "buzzing" the flowers, which releases the pollen (Buchmann 1974). NESTS OF Melissodes floris.--Although bees of the genus Melissodes are abundant in North and Central America, surprisingly little information is available regarding their nests. A complete nest of M. floris, which contained a dead female and three cells, is illustrated in figure 5. The 6-7 mm diameter horizontal burrow entered the bank at a 90 degree angle and then extended at about a 20 degree angle to the surface. The vertical cells, located at 9 to 13 cm from the nest entrance, had thin, shiny, fragile transparent linings. They measured 12 mm long by 6 mm wide; the necks were 4 mm wide. The burrow(s) leading to the cells had been filled with earth. The semiliquid, light-yellow provisions filled the lower 5 mm of the cells and supported the brood. Three dead female M. floris, each containing a puparium of a phorid fly in its thorax, were removed from other cells in the bank. Although the general nest structure resembles that of Melissodes obliqua Say (Custer 1928), there was no evidence that more than one female occupied the nest, nor were any other large nests found. Melissodes floris occurs from southern Mexico to Costa Rica (LaBerge 1961 and pers. comm.). NESTS OF Colletes sp.--A single complete nest of Colletes sp. nr. C. xerophilus was excavated, though several additional vacant cells of former nests were found while studying Centris. The nest and cells of this species most closely resemble those of Colletes compactus Cresson (Rozen and Favreau 1968) or C. thoracicus Smith (Batra, in preparation) in general arrangement and structure (fig. 4). The main burrow ran horizontally into the bank at 90 degree angle to the surface for 17 cm. Seven horizontal cells were found at the ends of 2 to 10 cm long earth-filled laterals on each side of the burrow. There was also an old, earth-filled cell 15 cm beyond the end of the main burrow. The cells, each containing a 2.0 x 0.7-mm egg suspended by its cephalic end from the ceiling, were composed of three distinct layers of transparent silky film, the outermost flimsy layer adhering to the soil. The 5 mm diameter cell closure also consisted of three layers. The semiliquid yellow provisions did not contact the eggs. Cells measured 11-12 mm long and 7.0 to 7.5 mm wide.
